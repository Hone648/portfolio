## The problem

A body-shop job ties together more than a customer and a vehicle. It has a repair order, approvals, estimates and supplements, communication history, attachments, an invoice, payments, and a closure state. When those records live on paper, the relationships live in memory. The risky failures are business decisions made with missing context: closing work that still has a balance due, or billing work that was never approved.

Unicos is implemented local-development software for keeping those decisions connected. It runs through Docker Compose against PostgreSQL, and it remains in active development rather than production operation.

The screens below show the current operating shape: a dashboard for shop-level work, a repair-order queue for active jobs, and a customer workspace that keeps people, vehicles, and jobs close together. The point is not a decorative interface pass. It is a way to make the job record visible enough that the next business decision can be made from the same context as the last one.

## Three engineering decisions

<ol aria-label="High-signal engineering decisions">
  <li>
    <strong>The repair order is the shared context</strong>
    <p>The repair order is the center of the model. A customer and vehicle establish who the work is for and what it is being done to; the repair order carries the job state, assignment, and lifecycle history. Estimates, supplements, communications, attachments, invoices, and payments then inherit context from the job instead of forming separate piles that must be reconciled by memory. Repair-order, estimate, and invoice numbers are generated on yearly application sequences rather than typed freely, reducing duplicate-number risk.</p>
  </li>
  <li>
    <strong>Rules and permissions follow the work</strong>
    <p>Lifecycle rules sit below the page layer so the same business checks apply when aligned callers attempt the same transition. A repair order cannot enter a final state while a non-void invoice still has a balance due. Estimates become read-only after submission in the ordinary workflow, and approval requires source and contact evidence. For technician-only users, repair-order access is limited to assigned jobs and related workflows inherit that job scope. Role checks and object-level job scope are implemented in the shop-facing flow, but this is not complete RBAC or a finished authorization system.</p>
  </li>
  <li>
    <strong>Billing stays explicit and transactional</strong>
    <p>Approval, conversion, issue, and payment are separate events. An approved estimate can become a draft invoice only when it has line items, approval evidence, no prior conversion, and a repair order without an invoice. Conversion copies line items inside a transaction and keeps the source estimate linked. Payment recording locks the invoice row, rechecks the remaining balance, and rejects overpayment rather than trusting a stale read. Unicos records invoices and payments at the application level; it is not a payment processor or an accounting ledger.</p>
  </li>
</ol>

## Sanitized architecture

The architecture is organized around a domain layer that owns workflow and billing rules, with presentation code calling through services instead of duplicating transition logic. The public diagrams are sanitized explanatory views derived from reviewed implementation evidence; they are not production infrastructure maps and do not expose private source details.

Those diagrams also show the boundary I care about most: UI pages can request an action, but they should not be the place where money and closure rules become true. The service layer is where the application checks whether a repair order can move, whether an estimate can be converted, and whether an invoice can accept another payment.

## Repair-order workflow

The repair-order workspace shows the job as a connected operating record. Communication entries are internal history, not outbound messaging. Attachments use local-development storage and metadata. Estimate screens show the approval path and supplement lineage, but they do not prove insurance-system integration or complete estimate versioning.

The billing flow intentionally keeps the stages apart. Creating an invoice from an approved estimate does not issue it, and issuing an invoice does not record a payment. That separation keeps correction possible: an issued invoice can be voided without erasing the approval decision that justified the work.

## Supporting operations

Billing, reporting, and administration are included because operational software needs more than the happy path. The invoice queue makes payment state visible. Reports are operational snapshots with selected CSV exports, not an accounting ledger or analytics platform. Django admin is available for development, trusted inspection, and emergency correction; it is not the normal shop workflow and it is not fully unified with the shop-facing authorization boundary.

## Active development boundary

Unicos runs locally with Docker Compose and PostgreSQL. Production hosting remains future work, including hardened settings, HTTPS, storage design, backup and restore, monitoring, and operational support. Attachment storage is local development persistence, estimate and invoice output goes through browser printing rather than generated PDFs, and there is no multi-shop tenancy, complete authorization model, production storage plan, backup regime, monitoring system, or real-shop operation claim.

## What this project demonstrates

Unicos shows how I turn a messy business workflow into application boundaries a database and service layer can defend. The important work is not just rendering repair-order screens; it is deciding where context lives, which transitions must be refused, where concurrency can corrupt money state, and how public evidence can show an active system without pretending it is finished infrastructure.
