## Why I built Unicos

I started Unicos after seeing how a friend who owns a body shop managed interconnected work through paper records.

A single job in that kind of shop pulls together more than it first appears: a customer and a vehicle, the work being performed and where it stands, an estimate and then supplements when the damage turns out worse than the first look suggested, who approved that additional work and when, photos and documents, a history of who was called about what, and eventually an invoice, payments against it, and a decision about whether the job is finished.

On paper, each of those lives on its own sheet and the connections live in somebody's memory. The failure mode is rarely a lost document — it is closing out a job that still has money owed on it, or invoicing work nobody approved.

Unicos is my attempt to keep those decisions connected. It is implemented working software rather than a design exercise, in active local development: it runs through Docker Compose against PostgreSQL, and production hosting is still ahead of it.

I defined the requirements and workflow constraints, made the architecture and domain decisions, reviewed and validated the changes, and kept final control over scope and merges.

## Making the repair order the shared context

The design decision everything else follows from is that the repair order is the record the rest of the system hangs on.

A customer and a vehicle establish who the work is for and what it is being done to, and the application validates that a vehicle belongs to the customer it is attached to. The repair order then establishes the job itself, carrying the workflow status, the assigned staff member, and the history of how that status has moved.

Everything downstream takes its context from there: estimates and supplements, communication entries recording internal history rather than outbound messaging, attachments, the invoice, and the payments recorded against it. That is what makes it possible to ask one coherent question — what is the state of this job — instead of assembling the answer from separate piles.

Repair-order, estimate, and invoice numbers are generated by the application on yearly sequences rather than typed in by staff, which is what keeps two people from creating the same document number on a busy afternoon.

## Turning status changes into business rules

A workflow status is easy to mistake for a label. It is really a set of claims about what is now allowed.

The clearest example in Unicos is closing a job. A repair order cannot move into a final state while a non-void invoice still has a balance due. In shop terms: you do not get to call the work finished while the customer still owes for it.

The tempting place to implement that is the page — grey out the button when there is a balance. It is not enough: a hidden button governs only the path that renders it, and any other route into the same transition, including an administrative one, is unaffected. So the rule lives in a centralized lifecycle service that checks it before applying any transition, and every aligned caller inherits it. A successful transition records status history, so how a job reached its current state stays answerable.

Estimates follow the same shape. A draft may be submitted; a submitted estimate may be approved, declined, or expired. Once an estimate leaves draft it becomes read-only in the ordinary workflow, because an estimate that can be quietly edited after submission is not a record of what was agreed. Approval is not just a status value either — the transition requires an approval source and contact before the server records the timestamp. Someone approved this, and the application insists on knowing who.

## Letting permissions follow the work

Authorization runs on two layers. The first is role-level: does this person get to reach this area or perform this kind of action at all? Authentication uses a custom staff user model, and named shop roles feed a centralized permission matrix that shop-facing views enforce on the server.

The second is object-level: even granted that, can they act on *this particular job*? A technician is the concrete case. A technician-only user can view the repair orders assigned to them and not unrelated work — and because estimates and billing take their scope from the repair order, that restriction follows into those workflows rather than leaving a separate unguarded door.

Django's `is_staff` flag is deliberately not treated as a shop-facing permission either; a user with no shop role has no application access beyond logging in.

This is real object-level authorization and it is not finished. Customer and vehicle object policies are incomplete, reporting lacks complete per-object scoping, admin authorization is not unified with the shop-facing policy helpers, and multi-shop tenancy is not implemented. It should not be called complete RBAC or complete object-level authorization, because it is neither yet.

## Moving an approved estimate into billing

The path from quoted work to money owed is where this application earns its keep, and what matters most is what it refuses to collapse.

An estimate starts as a draft, and its line items establish both the work and the total. Submission freezes ordinary editing; approval records where the approval came from and who gave it. Only then is an estimate eligible to become billing: it must be approved, carry that approval evidence, contain line items, not have been converted already, and belong to a repair order that does not already have an invoice.

Conversion creates a **draft** invoice, copies the estimate's line items across, and preserves the link back to the source estimate — all inside one database transaction, so a half-converted state is not reachable.

What conversion deliberately does not do is issue the invoice or record a payment. Approval is not billing, conversion is not issue, and issue is not payment — separate decisions made by different people at different times. Flattening them into one "complete billing" action would erase which ones actually happened, and make correction harder: while the stages stay distinct, an issued invoice can be voided without unwinding the approval that justified it.

A supplement is modeled as an estimate with a parent estimate from the same repair order, giving additional work its own line items and lifecycle — lineage, not a complete estimate-versioning system. Each repair order currently allows one invoice, which keeps billing rules comprehensible at the cost of supplement merging.

## Protecting billing changes at the database boundary

Billing mutations go through services that hold the rules: invoice issue and void, payment recording, and invoice line-item changes. Those services reject billing changes against a void invoice or a repair order already in a final state, require positive payment amounts, refuse overpayment, and keep the invoice's payment status synchronized with the payments actually recorded.

Most of that is ordinary validation. Payment recording needed something stronger.

Consider two payment requests arriving at nearly the same moment against one invoice. Both read the outstanding balance, both conclude their payment fits, and both write. The invoice is now overpaid, and every individual check passed on the way in — because each request validated against a balance that was accurate when read and stale by the time it was used.

The fix is to stop letting them read that number independently. Payment recording opens a transaction, locks the invoice row with `select_for_update()`, and only then rechecks eligibility and the remaining balance before saving. The second request waits, re-reads the balance after the first commits, and correctly rejects. Estimate conversion uses the same transaction-and-lock shape against the equivalent duplicate-conversion risk.

Unicos records invoices and payments at the application level; it is not a payment processor or an accounting ledger.

## Keeping the administration interface from bypassing the application

Django admin is useful for trusted inspection and for correcting data during development. It is not the operator workflow, and the problem it creates is specific: an administrative interface writes directly to models, so it can walk straight around the services holding every rule described above — the closure rule included.

The high-risk paths now either call the same domain services or are read-only: repair-order status, estimate status, invoice issue and void, payment creation, and invoice line items. Attachment upload metadata is protected from ordinary admin mutation.

That narrows accidental bypass; it does not unify the two. There is no complete admin object-policy integration, no audited override path, and some metadata remains directly editable — a separate trusted interface with acknowledged limits, not a second way to operate the application.

## How I delivered and validated the work

Development runs in narrow slices: an explicit problem, a bounded implementation, tests and migration checks, diff review, a pull request, and human-controlled acceptance.

Validation combines Ruff lint and formatting checks, Django system checks, migration-consistency verification, applying migrations, and running the test suite against PostgreSQL, then building the Docker image, starting the Compose environment, and checking the `/healthz/` endpoint — which confirms the local or CI process responds and is not monitoring. Manual browser review covers what automated checks read poorly: whether the right actions are available in a given workflow state. None of it establishes universal test coverage.

AI assistance was part of the workflow, helping with source inspection, implementation, test preparation, and diff review. Requirements, architecture, domain interpretation, acceptance, and merge decisions stayed mine.

## What active development means

Unicos runs locally through Docker Compose and remains in active development. Getting it to production is its own body of work, not a deployment step: hardened serving and settings, HTTPS and deployment configuration, a real design for media rather than local file storage, backup and restore operations, monitoring, and a stricter admin boundary.

Attachment storage is local development persistence, estimate and invoice output goes through browser printing, and reports are read-only filtered snapshots with selected CSV exports — adequate for the current stage, not production infrastructure.

## What this project demonstrates

Unicos is where I learned to take a business process that lives in someone's head and give it a shape a database can defend.

Building it meant choosing a central workflow record and making everything else derive context from it; putting lifecycle rules in services below the presentation layer, where every caller inherits them; combining role permissions with object-level scope so access follows the job; and keeping approval, conversion, issue, and payment as separate decisions rather than one convenient button. It meant reaching for transactions and row locks where concurrent requests could corrupt a balance, and going back to align an administrative interface that could bypass all of it.

It also meant staying clear about the line between a workflow implemented in depth and an application ready to be hosted and depended on. Unicos sits on the first side of that line, and I would rather say so than blur it.
